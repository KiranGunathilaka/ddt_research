The decisions in one screen

Fit four sensorsVelodyne Puck for cone positions, ZED2 for cone colour, one new IMU, one ZED-F9P rover. Wheel speeds and steering angle come from the car's CAN bus.
Leave three on the shelfThe two 2D lidars and the RealSense stay off the car. They add calibration and failure points without adding range or accuracy.
Buy littleOne industrial IMU, a CAN interface, power conversion and mounting. No wheel encoders, no steering sensor, no third RTK module yet.
Ask for the AGX OrinThe 64 GB Jetson AGX Orin is the right request. It is what the closest comparable FS-AI entry ran, and the car's 30 A supply carries it easily.
Build the simple stack firstLidar cones → 100 Hz odometry filter → 10 Hz cone-map SLAM → Delaunay mid-line → pure pursuit. This is the 2017 winning architecture.
Do not depend on RTKBase stations cannot be set up at the event, so RTK is a testing tool and an optional aid, never the thing the car steers by.

How this was put together: about 20 system papers and theses from 02_fsd_papers/ were searched for sensors, rates and fusion, and the passages quoted here were read in context; the 42 team code bases in 01_fsd_open_source_code/team_stacks/ were scanned by keyword for sensor drivers and estimator configuration; the ADS-DV limits come from the official documents in 03_fsuk_ddt_ads-dv/. Statements taken from a paper are stronger evidence than statements taken from a code scan, which shows that a driver or library is present, not that it raced. The two are kept in separate tables.

We command steering angle, front and rear axle torque (0–195 Nm) with a motor speed cap, and brake pressure in percent, each every 10 ms. The limits that shape the design:

  • Handshake within 50 ms. The VCU triggers the emergency brake if the AI computer does not echo the handshake bit in time (rule T1.1.8). The CAN bridge must be a small, separate, real-time-ish process that never waits on perception.
  • Power: 13.7 V nominal, 30 A fused, shared between the compute plate and the sensor plate. That is about 410 W for everything we fit.
  • Mass and space: a rear compute plate may weigh 8 kg including the plate. Compute can instead sit on the front sensor plate, which has the same power and CAN connector.
  • Sensor envelope: sensors must sit inside the surface defined by the top of the TSAL and the outer tyre edges, or within 100 mm of the bodywork. Teams on a shared IMechE car may not use the low zone ahead of the front tyres (T1.3.3–T1.3.4). Every sensor and the compute need IMechE approval through the ASF.
  • GNSS: “(D)GPS may be used but there will be no space to securely build up base stations on the competition site”, and wireless links are not guaranteed (T4.5).
  • No stored track knowledge in autocross. Data from a previous run may not be used, and no equipment is allowed on the track walk (D6.3). The car has to find the track itself.

The car is slow by Formula Student standards, which matters for every later choice. The 2026 specification gives 5 kW maximum electrical power, rear-wheel drive through an open differential, a 1.53 m wheelbase, and EUFS's CAN bridge uses 300 kg and a 0.253 m wheel radius. From those numbers, our estimate is about 2.5 m/s² off the line, power-limited above roughly 6.5 m/s, at most about 15 m/s at the end of the 75 m acceleration run, and 4–10 m/s on a track with 9 m hairpins. Treat these as estimates until we have driven it.

The common patterns

Eight things recur across these stacks. Each is followed by what it means for us.

1. A 3D lidar finds the cones; the camera names their colour

Roughly four in five of the teams in the tables above carry a 3D lidar, and every event winner among them does. The stated reason is range accuracy: a lidar return is good to a few centimetres at any distance, while stereo depth error grows with the square of distance. Vigo, the cleanest camera-only stack here, reports under 0.5 m error only out to 7 m. The lidar's weakness is colour. AMZ could classify colour from intensity only inside 4.5 m, so the camera supplies it.

The Velodyne VLP-16 is the most common single model: AMZ's 2017 winner, IIT Bombay and EUFS on the ADS-DV, Zurich UAS, Chalmers and FST Lisboa. IIT Bombay describes it as a “commonly utilized LiDAR unit by FS-AI teams”. Three of those pair it with a ZED camera, as we would.

For us: the Puck we already own is the proven entry-level choice, and the ZED2 gives colour. No lidar purchase is needed.

2. Every stack has an IMU and a speed measurement; ground-speed sensors are a top-team luxury

No stack runs without an inertial unit. SBG units appear in at least seven teams, Xsens and VectorNav in two each; most are full GNSS/INS units and many were sponsored. Wheel speeds are used everywhere they exist. An optical ground speed sensor appears only on high-power four-wheel-drive cars (AMZ, Elefant), where wheel slip makes wheel speed unreliable.

For us: buy an IMU. Wheel speeds come from CAN. A 5 kW rear-drive car has little slip, and its undriven front wheels have less, so a ground speed sensor would buy nothing.

3. Nobody steers by GNSS; RTK is a measuring instrument

AMZ won in 2017 with a consumer GPS and says so. In 2020 it used RTK only as ground truth to score its maps. Elefant uses RTK for testing, data collection and laying out tracks, and keeps a second speed source for “the likely case that the GNSS signal fails”. Only Vigo and TUW put their own base station in the localisation loop, which the FS-AI rules do not leave room for. Teams that want corrections at an event pull them over a cellular link (Elefant; NTRIP clients in the FEUP and FSLART code).

For us: the two F9P modules are valuable for testing and tuning. On the car the rover is an optional aid, mainly for velocity.

4. Two filters at two speeds

The strongest stacks split estimation in two. A fast filter turns IMU and speed data into a smooth velocity and short-term pose at 50–200 Hz. A slower mapper corrects that pose against cones at the lidar or camera rate. AMZ runs the first at 200 Hz and the second at 15 Hz. QUT measured the value of the split directly: predicting from a 5 Hz INS alone was the limit on its SLAM, and adding a 50 Hz IMU-and-wheel filter in front improved map accuracy by 65 %.

For us: this is the core of the proposed stack and the answer to the RTK-rate question below.

5. Map on the first lap, then localise

Cones are landmarks, so teams build a cone map while driving slowly, detect that the lap has closed, freeze the map and then drive faster against it. AMZ mapped at about 3 m/s until 2020. FastSLAM was the early choice (AMZ 2017–19, EUFS, MUR). GraphSLAM, usually through GTSAM or g2o, is the later one (AMZ 2020, KIT21d, FEUP, Carnegie Mellon, Chalmers, FSLART). Plain EKF-SLAM is still in use (QUT, FEUP, E-Team Pisa).

For us: trackdrive is ten laps and worth 250 of the 550 dynamic points, so mapping the first lap pays. Autocross is one lap with no stored data, so the exploratory mode must be able to finish a lap by itself.

6. Delaunay mid-line and pure pursuit first, MPC later

For an unknown track, teams triangulate the cones, take the mid-points of the edges that cross the track, and pick the most plausible chain as the centre line (AMZ, IIT Bombay, and the widely reused FaSTTUBe planner in our collection). The first controller is almost always pure pursuit: AMZ on its mapping lap, IIT Bombay, QUT, FEUP, MUR, FSLART. Stanley is the other simple choice. MPC arrives later and only once the map and vehicle model are trusted.

For us: pure pursuit with a PI speed loop is enough to score in all four dynamic events at ADS-DV speeds.

7. Jetson-class compute for most, a desktop PC for the fastest

A Jetson appears in about seventeen of the forty-two code bases. Teams running long-horizon MPC at 50 Hz use x86 with a desktop GPU: KIT an i7-9700K, Elefant a Ryzen 3700X with an RTX 2070 Super.

For us: the AGX Orin is in the mainstream. See Compute.

8. Delay is measured and compensated, not ignored

AMZ measured about 300 ms from cone detection to command and built delay compensation into both the velocity filter and the mapper. KIT de-skews each 10 Hz lidar sweep because “the distortion caused by the movement while scanning can not be neglected”. AMZ synchronises its two computers with chrony; Elefant uses PTP.

For us: stamp every measurement at acquisition and transform it with the pose from that instant. The latency budget shows what that is worth.

Is a 10–20 Hz RTK enough for the control loop?

Yes, because the control loop never reads the RTK. It reads a pose that is predicted every 10 ms from the IMU and the wheel speeds. The RTK, like the lidar, only has to correct that prediction before it drifts, and drift takes seconds to matter, not milliseconds.

The numbers behind that, at 10 m/s with an ordinary industrial MEMS IMU (gyro bias 0.05 °/s) and wheel speed good to 1 %:

Time since the last correctionDistance drivenAlong-track errorHeading errorWhat it means
10 ms (one filter step)0.10 m1 mm0.0005°Invisible.
50 ms (20 Hz RTK)0.5 m5 mm0.0025°Below the 1–2 cm noise of the RTK fix itself. Going from 10 Hz to 20 Hz gains nothing measurable.
100 ms (10 Hz RTK or lidar)1.0 m1 cm0.005°
1 s (ten missed corrections)10 m10 cm0.05°Still drivable in a 3 m wide track.
45 s (a whole lap blind)450 m4.5 m2.3°Not drivable. This is the drift the slow sensors exist to remove.

So the two rates answer two different questions. The prediction rate is set by how fast the car and its actuators respond, hence 100 Hz. The correction rate is set by how fast dead reckoning drifts, and anything from 5 Hz up is ample: QUT ran its GNSS at 5 Hz, EUFS models 5 Hz, AMZ's lidar and KIT's Pandar give 10 Hz. Run the F9P at 10 Hz with every constellation enabled. Its 20 Hz mode needs a reduced constellation set, which costs fix robustness for no benefit.

What does go wrong is tying the filter to the slow sensor. Vigo steps its EKF at 0.1 s “because the RTK has a maximum refresh rate of 10Hz”, so its pose is up to a metre old at 10 m/s. QUT started the same way and fixed it. The rule is: predict on the fastest sensor, correct with each slower one when it arrives, using its own timestamp.

Compute: the AGX Orin is the right request

Ask for the Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB developer kit. The reasons, in order of weight:

  • It is the proven choice for this exact job. IIT Bombay ran its FS-AI DDT entry on an AGX Orin with a VLP-16 and ZED2i cameras, which is our sensor set.
  • The workload fits. One YOLO network at 15–30 Hz on the GPU, lidar clustering, two small filters and a geometric controller leave most of the twelve cores idle. The GPU is the scarce resource, and the Orin's should be well ahead of the GTX 1060 in the IMechE in-car PC once the network runs under TensorRT. We have not benchmarked either.
  • It suits the car. At most 60 W from a 30 A supply, well under the 8 kg plate limit, and small enough to ride on the front sensor plate beside the sensors, which removes the long USB 3 run to the ZED2.
  • We keep it. IMechE recommends bringing your own compute because the in-car PCs are shared and access is restricted.

Latency budget

About 150 ms of software delay means a cone is 1.5 m closer than when it was scanned. Three things keep that harmless:

  1. Every detection is placed in the map using the pose at its own timestamp, so delay shifts when we learn about a cone, not where we think it is.
  2. The controller never waits for perception. It tracks the current path with a pose that is at most 10 ms old.
  3. The look-ahead point is kept beyond the distance covered in the actuator delay: at least 2.5 m, growing to about 8 m at 10 m/s.

Time and frames

  • One clock. Feed the F9P's PPS and NMEA time to the Velodyne interface box and to chrony on the Orin, so lidar packets and the system clock share GPS time. Stamp CAN frames with SocketCAN kernel timestamps. Give the ZED2 a fixed latency offset found by calibration.
  • Three frames. map → odom → base_link. The fast filter owns odom → base_link and is smooth but drifts. SLAM owns map → odom and jumps but does not drift. The map origin is the car's pose at the start line.

Build order

  1. Talk to the car. CAN bridge and state machine against the official API's simulate mode and the IMechE hardware-in-the-loop setup. IMechE plans a HiL CAN communications check as part of DDT scrutineering.
  2. Simulate. eufs_sim already models the ADS-DV with a VLP-16, a ZED, an IMU and a GPS.
  3. Odometry filter. Wheel speeds and IMU, scored against RTK on a figure of eight. Do not go further until a lap closes within about a metre without corrections.
  4. Lidar cones, mid-line, pure pursuit at 2–3 m/s. With no SLAM at all this already finishes autocross and trackdrive slowly.
  5. Camera colour and fusion.
  6. SLAM, lap closure, lap counting.
  7. Acceleration and skidpad modes.
  8. Speed. Fixed race line and speed profile after closure. MPC only if there is time left.

Open items

  • Own car or shared car? The mounting zones and the approval route differ. This page assumes a shared IMechE ADS-DV.
  • Lidar mounting height. Ring coverage of a cone depends on it. Work it out on the CAD in FS-AI_ADS-DV_CAD before the ASF.
  • Which Puck and which RealSense? A Puck Hi-Res has a ±10° field with 1.33° ring spacing and would extend the range figures above by half.
  • Wheel sensor resolution. Pulses per revolution are not in the documents we hold. Ask IMechE or count them on the car.
  • Steering actuator delay and rate limit. Measure a step response early; it sets the look-ahead.
  • Which vehicle specification is current? A 2019 PDF describes two motors and a lithium pack; the 2026 page describes 5 kW and lead-acid. The speed estimates here use the 2026 page.
  • F9P update rates. The constellation limits for 20 Hz RTK should be checked against the integration manual for our firmware.
