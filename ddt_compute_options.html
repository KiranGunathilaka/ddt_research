Which computer for the ADS-DV

6 November 2026 · Falcon FS-AI DDT entry on the IMechE ADS-DV · part of the DDT architecture and test plan · results are written as rank, year, dynamic events finished, e.g. R6, 2026, 3/4 (FSG Driverless Cup unless marked)

The lead candidate is the Advantech MIO-5379 with the Intel Core Ultra 7 255H, the board KIT raced in 2026 (R6, 2026, 3/4). It is an industrial single-board computer: rated for vibration, fed straight from 12–24 V, with two CAN-FD ports on the board, though those have no public Linux driver yet (below), so a USB CAN adapter carries the car’s CAN for now. It has no NVIDIA GPU. That is enough because the lidar finds the cones and the camera only has to say what colour they are, which the chip’s own graphics and NPU can do.

What the computer has to do, and where it has to fit

NeedDetail
Run our stackThe main lidar (a Hesai ATX is planned; the VLP-16 is owned) on Ethernet, the ZED2 on USB 3, the IMU and F9P on USB, the car on CAN. Lidar cone detection, cone SLAM, and the planner and controller at 50–100 Hz. The camera gives cone colour only (below), so the stack must run without an NVIDIA GPU.
Run off the carThe compute plug gives 13.7 V nominal, 30 A fused, shared with the sensor plate. A 7W2 combo D-sub carries the power and the vehicle CAN (500 kbit/s, already terminated); teams supply their own CAN interface (FS-AI_Compute README). The sensor socket on the same 12 V system is quoted at 11.5–14.5 V (ADS-DV CAD README).
Fit the carAt the rear, about 280 × 200 × 200 mm and at most 8 kg including the mounting plate. “The mounting plate will be mounted rigidly to the vehicle chassis. Teams should consider the need for Anti-Vibration (AV) mounts” (reference compute installation). It can also go on the front sensor plate, which has the same plug.
Have a fallbackEvery shared car can carry an InCarPC: i7-8700T, GTX 1060, 32 GB, PEAK CAN, removable 2.5-inch SSD caddy. It is shared between teams, so we want our own, but our x86 image should also boot on it. The GTX 1060 is SM 6.1, below TensorRT 10’s SM 7.5 minimum (TensorRT support matrix), so a CPU or OpenVINO build is the one that runs there unchanged.

What team code uses a GPU for

All 42 team code bases in our archive were searched for CUDA source files (.cu), CUDA in CMake, TensorRT, PyTorch on cuda, ONNX Runtime’s CUDA provider, OpenCV CUDA, CuPy, OpenVINO and the ZED SDK (my script; libraries bundled under third_party and similar folders were skipped). Public repositories are often older than what a team races, so this shows what the software needs, not what every car runs today.

The camera without an NVIDIA GPU

What the ZED SDK adds

Stereolabs’ ZED SDK is two things: the ZED camera’s official driver, and its depth, tracking and object engine. It needs CUDA for both, even when depth is switched off. Without an NVIDIA GPU the camera runs through zed-open-capture, Stereolabs’ open library that grabs the stereo video and the ZED2’s IMU over plain USB. Images are rectified with OpenCV and the camera’s factory calibration file, and OpenCV can compute stereo depth on the CPU if it is ever wanted.

How a coloured cone gets onto the map

A cone map is a list of cones, each with a position and a colour estimate. Every new sighting is turned into a position in the car frame, moved into the map with the car’s pose, matched to the nearest map cone by position, and its colour is added as a vote. Colour never decides which cone is which; it rides along with a sighting that already has a position. AMZ feeds lidar and camera sightings into one SLAM map this way, each carrying a position and a colour probability (Kabzan 2019, pp. 21–22). The question is only where the position comes from:

Colour without YOLO

YOLO is a detector: it searches the whole image for cones and draws their boxes. When the lidar has already found the cone, only the colour of a small patch at a known pixel is needed, and simpler methods do that:

  • Colour thresholds. Technion’s classical cone code has HSV and RGB classifiers and template matching with k-means (ClassicConeDetection README). MUR Melbourne made blue, yellow and orange colour masks in MATLAB (YoloColorParse).
  • A small classifier on the patch. MUR trains a PyTorch classifier on 32 × 32 lidar intensity crops of blue and yellow cones and exports it to ONNX (lidar-cone-classifier README). The same size of network on camera crops is tiny work for OpenVINO on the 255H’s graphics or NPU.
  • Rules versus a network. On AMZ’s lidar stripes, a rule on the brightness gradient and a CNN were both 96% accurate on nearby cones of the type they were built on, but the rule failed on cones with an extra FSG sticker while the CNN was hardly affected (Kabzan 2019, p. 16). So start with HSV thresholds and keep a small CNN ready for odd lighting and stickers.

The processors

How to read the names. In Core Ultra 7 255H, “Ultra” is Intel’s line with an NPU built in, “7” the tier, “2” Series 2 (Arrow Lake, 2025), “55” the model and “H” a 28 W high-performance laptop chip. The 155H is Series 1 (Meteor Lake, late 2023); Advantech also sells the MIO-5379 with it. In i7-1370PRE, “13” is the 13th generation (Raptor Lake, 2023), “P” a 28 W thin-laptop chip, and “RE” Intel’s real-time embedded version with industrial temperatures and Time Coordinated Computing (Intel). The i7-14700 is a 65 W desktop chip in a socket; “F” would mean no graphics, “T” a 35 W version. Laptop chips are soldered to the board and are not sold on their own.

The processor, network and AI parts run on stock Ubuntu; the board-specific parts hang off Advantech’s EIO-300 embedded controller and depend on Advantech’s software. Community. No public forum threads or bug reports for the MIO-5379 under Linux turned up, good or bad. Advantech’s eio-300 driver repository has one release (V1.0.2, 31 December 2025), no stars and no issues, so few people outside Advantech use it. KIT is the only Formula Student team known to run the board; it lists an STM32 next to it, which may be its CAN link, though that is not confirmed. The community that does exist is for the processor: the 255H is a 2025 laptop chip, and everything on its side (graphics, NPU, Ethernet, USB, NVMe) is in the mainline kernel. The risk sits only in the parts behind the EIO-300.

Supplier. Advantech is active on Linux: its SUSI repository gets new packages several times a week (commits on 29 October and on 2 and 3 November 2026), Canonical certified its MIC-770 V3W on Ubuntu 24.04 (Canonical), and it is sending controller drivers upstream. The MIO-5379 itself is not Ubuntu-certified. Support runs through the distributor or Advantech’s field engineers; the manual’s BIOS chapter refers a missing CPU patch code to “an Advantech application engineer” (manual, p. 43).

So: install Ubuntu 24.04 with the HWE kernel, run the stack in our Docker image, and bring the car’s CAN in through a USB CAN adapter with a mainline SocketCAN driver, as the old custom-PC plan did. Before ordering, ask Advantech in writing for a Linux driver for the Nuvoton CAN-FD (ideally SocketCAN), or the register description so we could write one, and for a 24.04 SUSI package.

Setting it up

It is an ordinary x86 PC with a UEFI BIOS (AMI Aptio). Nothing has to be flashed or programmed before Ubuntu goes on, and the code is built and run as on any Ubuntu machine.

  1. Fit the RAM, the NVMe SSD and the cooler (the thermal pad has a plastic film to peel off first; manual, p. 16).
  2. Connect an HDMI monitor and a USB keyboard, power on and press Del. The setup prompt waits 1 s by default, so tap the key from power-on (p. 43, p. 79).
  3. Start the Ubuntu 24.04 USB stick from Save & Exit → Boot Override (p. 80) and install to the SSD.
  4. Set the BIOS for the car (table below), then work over SSH.

The manual does not describe BIOS updates. The BIOS has a built-in EFI shell (p. 79), which is where flash tools usually run; ask Advantech for the update file and tool if one is ever needed. The manual’s screenshots show a BIOS built on 1 July 2025.

Moving the laptop’s Ubuntu SSD. It will normally boot as it is. Ubuntu installs a fallback boot loader at EFI/BOOT/bootx64.efi (the laptop has it, with fbx64.efi), which the new BIOS finds without a boot entry, and the drive’s mounts are by UUID. Points to check:

  • the drive has to be an M.2 2280 NVMe; the M-key slots take PCIe x4 NVMe (p. 11)
  • keep the HWE kernel, 6.14 or newer, for the Arc graphics and the NPU; the laptop already runs 7.0
  • network interface names change with the hardware, so a static address for the lidar port has to be set again; give the board its own hostname so it does not clash with the laptop on the team network
  • a drive with TPM-backed encryption asks for its recovery key on a new board; a passphrase or no encryption moves without trouble
  • the laptop’s own drivers and tools come along; they do no harm, but they are clutter

A moved drive is fine for a first test. For the car, a fresh install from a USB stick is cleaner.

The Jetsons we already have

Jetson Nano as a ZED SDK box

The Nano can run the ZED SDK next to the industrial PC: the ZED2 on the Nano’s USB, the Nano running depth and a detector, and only a small cone list (position, colour, time) sent to the PC over ROS 2 on its own Ethernet port. It is the same split NTNU uses (R2, 2026, 4/4), with a 2019 Nano in place of an Orin NX:

  • Frozen software. JetPack 4.6.6 is the last release for the Nano (NVIDIA), and Stereolabs points Nano users to the ZED SDK 4.0 build for L4T 32.7 (Stereolabs forum). The host is Ubuntu 18.04, so ROS 2 Humble runs in a container such as dustynv’s L4T r32.7 images; our stack is Humble too.
  • Send results, not video. The SDK’s own video streaming needs the SDK, and so an NVIDIA GPU, on the receiving side.
  • Tight compute. One 128-core Maxwell GPU (about 0.47 TFLOPS FP16) and 4 GB shared between depth and the detector. Stereolabs’ only Nano figure is about 30 fps of depth at 720p in PERFORMANCE mode, from 2019 with the first ZED (Stereolabs); the 255H’s graphics alone are about ten times the Nano on paper.
  • Hardware. A 12 → 5 V 4 A supply, a microSD boot card to lock in place, no vibration rating, and chrony between the Nano and the PC so camera cones line up with lidar scans.

With the lidar giving positions, none of this is needed for colour. The Nano fits the 1:10 car in stage 3, or a later camera-only backup if the rig shows one is worth it.

The Xavier you can borrow

The AGX Xavier stays on JetPack 5.1.x (Ubuntu 20.04, CUDA 11.4, TensorRT 8.5), since JetPack 6 does not support it (NVIDIA forum). Its 9–20 V input runs from 12 V (NVIDIA forum), and Zurich UAS Racing raced almost our sensor kit on one: a VLP-16, a ZED 2i and an SBG Ellipse-D (Finn 2025). It is a free rig computer while the car computer is bought, not the car computer: it is Arm, next to an x86 InCarPC fallback.

Recommendation

  1. Buy the MIO-5379C7P-R1A1U (LKR 808,977 with RAM, a 1 TB SSD, the plug and the CANable, at the 2–8 November rates). It matches the layout of the 2026 winner and of KIT: an industrial Intel board, no GPU, the lidar finding the cones. It is rated for vibration, runs straight from the car’s supply, and has two Ethernet ports on the board. Bring the car’s CAN in through the CANable 2.0 until Advantech supplies a Linux driver for the on-board CAN-FD; ask for one before ordering.
  2. Use the ZED2 for colour only, through zed-open-capture and the lidar projection above; start with HSV thresholds and keep a small CNN on OpenVINO ready.
  3. Check on the stage 2a rig: lidar detection and SLAM CPU load at the lidar’s scan rate; colour latency per scan on the Arc GPU and NPU; a full-image YOLO fallback on OpenVINO against the 25 ms budget; power and temperature in the closed enclosure with its fan; the USB CAN adapter against the ADS-DV CAN at 500 kbit/s; Ethernet link drops on the i226 lidar port.
  4. If the rig shows the camera needs the full ZED SDK, add a Jetson Orin NX 8 GB next to the MIO-5379 for the camera alone (LKR 448,341). Only if the whole stack turns out to need a GPU, switch to the AIMB-279 with the i7-14700 and an RTX 5050 in a real slot (LKR 750,505), not the MIO-5379 with an MXM card.
  5. Keep the Jetson Nano for the 1:10 car and the Xavier for the rig until the board arrives.
