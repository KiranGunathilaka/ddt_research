Lidar resolution, field of view and SLAM

5 November 2026 · Falcon FS-AI DDT entry on the IMechE ADS-DV · the theory behind the lidar choice. The ray-cast simulator is on Lidar Coverage and Hairpins.

The question: for a car that builds a map of cones and localises against it, as Falcon's stack will, is a 120° lidar enough, and is the Hesai ATX's 256 channels a real advantage over a spinning lidar? This page answers from geometry and estimation theory. Every figure is computed in the page from the formulas shown, so changing an input changes the result. Where the theory goes against the 120° lidar, the page says so.

Short answer
  • 256 channels are an advantage, and the reason is beam spacing, not channel count. The ATX’s beams are 0.08° apart vertically, 25 times closer than the VLP-16’s 2°. A cone is 32.5 cm tall. Past 9.3 m a 2° spacing leaves gaps wider than the cone, so whole bands of range get no ray at all. On an unknown track (autocross, and lap 1 of trackdrive) how far ahead the car confirms cones sets how fast it can approach a hairpin.
  • It is not an advantage for map precision. Once any of these lidars detects a cone, one scan puts its centre within 2.5 cm (Figure 4).
  • 120° is enough in theory, with one weak spot. Mid-turn in any hairpin under 13 m outside diameter, the inside edge is outside a 120° view from the ADS-DV’s front plate (Figure 5). Those cones were mapped on the approach, so the planner still has them. Localisation is the weak spot: in a 9 m hairpin with cones at the worst legal spacing, the ATX alone sees fewer than 2 cones for 4.0 m. Odometry carries the car across that stretch, with a predicted drift of about 9 cm against a 1.5 m margin (Figure 6).
  • What would prove this wrong is listed under Verdict, with the test for each. The VLP-16 we own removes the weak spot without buying anything, at the cost of time sync and calibration.

The two numbers, explained

An earlier reply gave two columns: “2 rings guaranteed out to” and “3+ rays on a cone out to”. Here is what each means.

  • Ray: one laser shot. If it hits the cone it returns one point.
  • Ring: one channel, a laser fixed at one elevation, swept across in azimuth. On a cone it leaves a short row of points at one height. The number of rings on a cone is the number of different heights the cone is sampled at.
  • Beam gap at the cone: the vertical distance between two neighbouring channels at the cone’s range \(r\). For channels \(\Delta\theta\) apart:
\[ g(r) = r\,\big(\tan\theta_{k+1}-\tan\theta_k\big)\;\approx\; r\,\Delta\theta \]

At 10 m that is 35 cm for the VLP-16 (2°), 24 cm for the OS1-32 (1.37°), 5.8 cm for the OS1-128 (0.33°) and 1.4 cm for the ATX (0.08°, its 256 channels spread evenly over 20°).

  • Rings guaranteed. A cone of height \(H = 0.325\) m spans an interval that beams \(g\) apart cross either \(\lfloor H/g\rfloor\) or \(\lfloor H/g\rfloor + 1\) times, depending on where the beams happen to fall on it. That depends on the cone’s range, the car’s pitch and bumps in the ground, none of which we control, so the number to design with is the lower one:
\[ m_{\min}(r)=\Big\lfloor \frac{H}{g(r)} \Big\rfloor \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m_{\min}\ge 2 \iff r \le \frac{H}{2\tan\Delta\theta} \]

That is the “2 rings guaranteed out to” column: 4.7 m for the VLP-16, 6.8 m for the OS1-32, 28 m for the OS1-128 and 119 m for the ATX. Past \(H/\tan\Delta\theta\) not even one ring is guaranteed: 9.3 m for the VLP-16.

  • Rays on the cone = rings × points per ring. A ring at height \(z\) crosses a cone of radius \(\rho(z)=r_0-(r_0-r_1)\,z/H\) (\(r_0\) = 114 mm at the base, \(r_1\) = 25 mm at the top), and columns \(\Delta\phi\) apart put about \(2\rho(z)/(r\,\Delta\phi)\) points on it. The “3+ rays” column used the threshold of the coverage page’s detector, which is also the minimum ISC uses with its ATX (cone_detection.py:91-92).
A correction to that reply. Its “3+ rays out to” column was the farthest range at which a cone gets 3 rays in the coverage page’s ray-cast. That ray-cast has the car level and the cone centred on a laser column, the most favourable sideways position. For the spinners this overstates the reach. Allow the cone to sit anywhere between two columns, and the car to pitch up to ±1°, and the Helios 1615’s 20 m becomes 11.75 m; the OS1-32’s 19.6 m becomes 11.25 m. Figure 2 shows all three versions. This page’s model reproduces the coverage page’s figures to within 0.1 m when it uses the same favourable placement.

Rays against range: a cliff, not a slope

While several rings land on a cone, its expected ray count is just its silhouette area over the solid angle of one ray. With angles in radians:

\[ \bar n(r)\;=\;\frac{(r_0+r_1)\,H}{r^{2}\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi} \qquad\text{e.g. ATX at 20 m: } \frac{0.0452}{400\times 1.37{\times}10^{-3}\times 1.40{\times}10^{-3}} \approx 59 \]

So doubling the range quarters the rays, and halving either spacing doubles them. That formula only holds while \(g(r) \ll H\). Once the beam gap passes the cone height, the count does not shrink smoothly. It jumps between zero and one thin ring as the cone moves through the beam pattern. Figure 2 shows the worst case (solid) beside the level-car curves.

What matters is angular density, not channel count. The Helios 5515 has the same 32 channels as the 1615 but spreads them over 70° of which half point at the car or the ground near it, so it does worse at range. The OS2-128 puts its 128 channels into 22.5° instead of the OS1-128’s 42.4° and reaches about 45% farther.

The pitch envelope is the honest knob. The ADS-DV pitches under braking and acceleration, and a test track is not flat: a 1% change in slope is 0.57°. At ±1° every lidar here sees every vertical phase of its beam pattern, so the solid curve is what the car can count on. ISC’s pipeline, on an ATX, rejects any cone farther than 20 m as “too sparse / unreliable” (cone_detection.py:69-70). This model gives the ATX 56 rays on a cone at 20 m, so geometry is not the reason. Their unit may have run a sparser mode, or the trouble may be in ground removal at range; the code does not say.

What landmark SLAM needs from one scan

A landmark SLAM never builds a dense point-cloud map. Every scan becomes a list of cone detections, each with a position and an uncertainty. Each detection is matched to a cone already in the map or starts a new one, and the matched ones correct the car’s pose. EKF-SLAM and factor graphs both work this way; ISC’s factor graph is an example (factor_graph.py). So the idea that a sparse lidar has to drive past a cone to build up its cluster belongs to dense mapping, not to a cone map. What a cone map needs from each scan is three things:

  1. Detection: enough rays to form a cluster. Teams’ minimums: ISC 3 with the ATX (cone_detection.py:91-92); QUT 2, with the comment “2-3 allows for long-range cone detection” (lidar_detector.yaml:16); OSU 3 (config.yaml:40); Hydrakon 3 (lidar_cluster.py:36); MUR 10 (cluster_params.yaml:2).
  2. Verification: evidence the cluster is a cone and not a post, a tyre wall, a shoe or ground clutter. One ring gives an arc about a cone’s width, which many things produce. Two or more rings show the width shrinking with height, the taper that only a cone has. ISC fits a cone template to each cluster and rejects poor fits (cone_fit.py), which needs points at more than one height. The same goes for colour: the stripe shows up in lidar intensity only across several rings, and AMZ found that “the sparsity of the point cloud combined with the small size of the cones limits the color estimation range to 4.5 m” (Kabzan 2019, p. 11).
  3. Precision: how far off the detected centre is. In the SLAM this is the measurement noise, the \(\sigma_i\) below.

The measurement model, for cone \(i\) at map position \(m_i\) seen from a car at \(p\) with heading \(\psi\):

\[ z_i = R(\psi)^{\top}(m_i-p) + v_i,\qquad v_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_i^{2}I) \]

Each scan that sees a cone adds information to it, so after \(N\) scans the cone’s position covariance is

\[ P_N^{-1} = P_0^{-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_j^{-2} I ,\qquad N \approx \frac{f\,(r_{\text{det}}-r_{\text{use}})}{v} \]

where \(f\) is the scan rate, \(r_{\text{det}}\) the range at which the cone is first detected and \(r_{\text{use}}\) the range at which the planner needs it. Speed cuts \(N\); a longer detection range adds it back. Figure 4 measures \(\sigma\) for one scan by simulation: the cone is placed at random over pitch and sideways position, 2 cm of range noise is added to every point, and a circle of the cone’s known radius at each point’s height is fitted to the points.

What Figure 4 shows: once a cone is detected, every lidar places it within a few centimetres; on a 3 m track that is far more precise than the planner or data association needs. The lidars differ in whether the cone is detected at all, and that drops off a cliff at the ranges in Figure 2. So a dense lidar does not buy a more precise map. It buys earlier, surer detections.

Real pipelines weight detections more cautiously than this simulation, because mixed returns at cone edges, ground-removal leftovers and partial occlusion add error it leaves out. ISC tells its SLAM \(\sigma = 1.5\,(0.05+0.005\,r)\,/\sqrt{n/10}\) for a cone with \(n\) points (cone_detection.py:456-457). At 20 m that is 41 cm for 3 points and 9 cm for 60, so in their map a 3-point detection counts about 20 times less (the ratio of the inverse variances). By ISC’s own weighting, then, a dense scan is worth far more per frame.

Why stacking several sparse scans is not the same thing. To merge scans you shift each by the car’s estimated motion, so a heading error \(\delta\psi\) smears a cone \(r\,\delta\psi\) sideways: half a degree at 15 m is 13 cm, more than half the 22.8 cm base. Worse, the merged cluster is built from the pose estimate, so its error is correlated with that estimate. Both an EKF and a factor graph assume measurement noise is independent of the state. Feeding back stacked detections counts the same information twice and makes the filter overconfident. A single dense scan is an independent measurement, as the theory requires.

How far ahead the car must see

On an unknown track the car must confirm the cones of a corner before the point where it has to start braking for it. Adding up the distances covered at speed \(v\): the pipeline latency \(t_{\text{lat}}\), \(M\) confirming scans at rate \(f\), braking from \(v\) to the corner speed \(\sqrt{a_{\text{lat}}R}\) at deceleration \(a_b\), plus a distance \(d_{\text{look}}\) into the corner to see its shape:

\[ d_{\text{req}}(v)= v\Big(t_{\text{lat}}+\frac{M}{f}\Big)+\max\!\Big(0,\;\frac{v^{2}-a_{\text{lat}}R}{2a_b}\Big)+d_{\text{look}} \]

A lidar whose guaranteed detection range is \(r_{\text{det}}\) allows speeds up to the \(v\) where \(d_{\text{req}}(v)=r_{\text{det}}\). The defaults: 0.3 s latency, about 150 ms of software plus 150 ms of steering actuation (Sensors and Autonomy Stack, latency budget); 3 confirming scans at 10 Hz; ISC’s 3 m/s² lateral limit (control_node.py:324-340) round the ADS-DV’s 4 m minimum radius; 4 m/s² braking (an assumption to measure on the car); 5 m look into the corner. Our own estimate puts the ADS-DV at 4–10 m/s on a track with 9 m hairpins and about 15 m/s at most (Sensors and Autonomy Stack), shaded in Figure 3.

Why a 120° lidar loses the inside of a tight turn

Put the car on the centreline of a constant-radius turn, rear axle on radius \(R_c\), with the lidar \(L\) metres ahead of the rear axle. The car’s heading is the tangent at the rear axle. From the lidar, the inside edge (radius \(R_i=R_c-w/2\)) is nearest the heading direction along the line that just touches it, so the smallest bearing at which any inside cone can appear is

\[ \beta_{\min}= 90^\circ+\arctan\frac{L}{R_c}-\arcsin\frac{R_i}{\sqrt{R_c^{2}+L^{2}}} \]

The inside edge can be seen mid-turn only if \(\beta_{\min}\le \text{FoV}/2\). On the ADS-DV the plate is 0.425 m ahead of the wheelbase midpoint, so \(L\) = 1.19 m (coverage page). An 11 m hairpin on a 3 m track gives \(\beta_{\min}\) = 69.8°, beyond the ATX’s 60°, which matches the ray-cast’s 0 of 7 inside cones in view through the turn. The forward mount causes this: with the lidar over the rear axle (\(L=0\)) a 120° view would see the inside edge in every legal hairpin.

This does not make the hairpin unplannable. The inside cones are in view on the approach and stay in the map: in the coverage page’s ray-cast all 7 inside cones of the 11 m hairpin are in the map at turn-in, and in the worst legal placement the single inside cone is too (coverage page). FaSTTUBe’s planner, which ISC uses unchanged, plans on map cones and adds virtual cones for an edge it cannot see (what the code does). The open question is localisation, next.

Staying localised through the hairpin

Localising against a known map means solving for three unknowns, \(x\), \(y\) and \(\psi\). Each cone seen gives two equations (its offset ahead and to the side). One cone leaves one combination unobservable: the car could be anywhere on a circle round that cone, turned to keep it at the same bearing. Two or more cones not on one line from the car fix all three. The best precision any unbiased estimator can reach from one scan is the Cramér–Rao bound:

\[ J=\sum_i \frac{H_i^{\top}H_i}{\sigma_i^{2}},\qquad H_i=\Big[\,-R^{\top}\;\;\; \tfrac{\partial R^{\top}}{\partial\psi}(m_i-p)\Big],\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat x,\hat y,\hat\psi)\succeq J^{-1} \]

with \(\sigma_i\) set by ISC’s weighting above, so cones with few points count for less. Figure 6 uses the coverage page’s ray-cast for which cones each lidar sees at every point of three hairpins. The worst legal placement is cones exactly 5 m apart even in the corner (cones specification DE6.3.1, p. 1).

Figure 6. Cones in view and pose precision through a hairpin

Left: top view. Filled cones are seen with at least 3 rays by the chosen lidars at this point; the ellipse is the pose bound, drawn 10 times larger. Right: cones in view along the run, from 16 m before turn-in. Shading marks where the ATX alone sees fewer than 2.

Through a gap with fewer than 2 cones, the pose is carried by odometry: wheel speeds for distance, the gyro for heading. Its error grows with the gap. With a wheel-odometry scale error \(\epsilon\), gyro scale error \(s_g\) and gyro bias \(b_g\), over a gap of length \(d\) round a turn of radius \(R\) at speed \(v\):

\[ e_{\parallel}\approx \epsilon\,d,\qquad \delta\psi\approx s_g\,\frac{d}{R}+b_g\,\frac{d}{v},\qquad e_{\perp}\approx \frac{d\,\delta\psi}{2} \]

When cones come back into view, each must still be matched to the right map cone. That fails only if the drift approaches half the distance between neighbouring cones, at least 1.5 m across a 3 m track.

What the 256 channels cost

  • Processing. The ATX produces 3.84 million points a second (Hesai), 13 times the VLP-16’s 300,000. Our stack must run on the CPU without the GPU. Most of those points are ground near the car. ISC crops the ATX cloud at 25 m before clustering because the ~174,000 returns per frame make clustering “the dominant cost” (cone_detection.py:72-78). The reach in Figure 2 only helps if the far field is processed: a narrow band of rows just below the horizon covers everything past 25 m, so it can be handled separately and cheaply.
  • Near blind zone. The ATX’s lowest beam is −10° from 0.57 m up, so it cannot see the top of a cone within about 1.4 m. Its 20° vertical view also leaves little margin for a badly tilted mount.
  • Field of view. The weak spot in Figure 6.
  • Price. About $1,540 for one ATX on Alibaba. The spinners with comparable spacing are the OS1-128 (≈ $17,700) and OS2-128 (from $10,000), far over the $2,000 budget. A 32-channel Helios is $2,550, also over, and Figure 2 shows it barely beats the VLP-16 at range. Prices and sources are in the 2026 field tree.

Conclusion. Within the budget, the ATX is the better single lidar for this car. The theory supports both halves of the claim:

  • The 256 channels are an advantage. Their close vertical spacing keeps every cone the car will race towards sampled by several rings, which the spinners in budget cannot do past 5–10 m. That sets how fast the car may approach a hairpin it has never seen.
  • 120° is enough, as long as the SLAM keeps every mapped cone and can ride odometry for up to about a second in the tightest hairpins.

Adding the VLP-16 makes no difference ahead, but it closes the localisation gap in the turn. It is insurance whose cost is engineering time, not money.

What would prove this wrong, and the test for each:

  1. The ATX puts far fewer rays on a cone at 20–30 m than this model. This is ISC’s “too sparse” comment. Test: with the sensor rig, count points on a cone at 10, 20 and 30 m.
  2. Odometry drifts much more than 1–2% plus 1° per half turn. Test: drive a 4 m arc in a car park and compare odometry with the RTK track.
  3. The SLAM declares a failure, or mismatches cones, after a second with fewer than 2 in view. This is the failure ISC hit on 12 May 2026 (coverage page). Test: run our SLAM in simulation through the worst 9 m hairpin with the ATX alone.
  4. The far field cannot be processed in time on the CPU. Test: time cone detection on recorded ATX frames with and without the far band.

If test 2 or 3 fails, mount the VLP-16 as well; if test 1 fails, the reach argument for the ATX weakens and the comparison should be redone with the measured counts.

Assumptions

  • Cone: the small FS cone, 228 mm base, 325 mm tall, modelled as a frustum with a 50 mm top. Flat ground; the cone straight ahead of the lidar for Figures 1–4.
  • Lidars as on the coverage page: the ATX’s 256 channels spread evenly over ±10° (the real unit packs them closer near the middle, 0.05° at the finest, so its reach at the horizon is if anything longer), 0.08° columns, 0.57 m up; the spinners 0.70 m up, VLP-16 and Helios at 0.2°, the Ousters at 2048 columns (0.18°). The car’s own body is ignored here; the coverage page includes it.
  • Pitch: all channels tilt together; ±1° covers every vertical phase for every lidar here.
  • Detection range is purely geometric. Every lidar here is rated beyond 90 m on a 10% target, so return energy is not the limit inside 80 m.
  • Figure 4’s error leaves out mixed returns at cone edges, ground-removal leftovers and occlusion, so it is a lower bound. ISC’s weighting is the field-tuned alternative and is used in Figure 6.
  • Figure 6’s cones in view come from the coverage page’s ray-cast, which includes the car body and cones hiding each other. The pose bound uses only the current scan, with the map known.
